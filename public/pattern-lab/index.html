What the CogAT is

THE SHORT VERSION

The Cognitive Abilities Test is a reasoning test many schools give in elementary and middle school, often as one input into a gifted or accelerated-placement decision. It is not a test of what your child has been taught this year. There is no history unit to review and no vocabulary list to memorise — it asks how well someone works out an unfamiliar pattern.

It has three parts, called batteries: Verbal, Quantitative and Nonverbal. Each battery holds three question types, so nine in total. Children sit a level matched to their age, not to their grade’s curriculum.

Most children have never seen questions shaped like these before the morning of the test. That is the honest reason practice helps at all: not because it teaches reasoning, but because an unfamiliar format costs time and confidence that have nothing to do with how well a child thinks.

The difficulty adapts as you go

Each of the nine skills has its own working level. Answer a Number Series question correctly and the next one is harder; miss one and the next is easier. Because the nine move independently, a child who finds figures easy and word analogies hard ends up with a session that reflects that, instead of one flat difficulty for everything.

Every question explains its own pattern

After each answer, Pattern Lab says what the rule actually was — the constant step in a number series, the shared property in a set of figures, the relationship the analogy turned on. This is the part that makes an unfamiliar format familiar, and it is why the explanation appears on correct answers too.

Choose how long you have

Three session lengths: nine questions (about five minutes, one per skill), eighteen, or the full twenty-seven. Longer sessions produce a more reliable picture, and each skill is labelled by how much evidence sits behind it rather than pretending three questions and one question mean the same thing. You can stop part way through and still see results for what you answered.

What Pattern Lab is not

PLAINLY

Not the CogAT itself

It is not made by the people who publish the CogAT. Every question here is original. None is taken from a real test.

Not a score prediction

A practice session under no time pressure, at a kitchen table, cannot tell you what a child will do in a proctored test. Any site that tells you otherwise is guessing.

Not a study plan

Reasoning is not a topic that can be crammed. What familiarity with the format removes is the first-five-minutes confusion, and that is all it is offered as.

Not a diagnosis

Nine short question sets are a snapshot of one afternoon, not a measure of a child.

If you want the official picture, your child’s school is the place to ask. Ask them which level is being given, when, and how the result will be used.
