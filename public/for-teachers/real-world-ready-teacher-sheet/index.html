Real World Ready Challenge — Teacher Sheet

24 scenarios · 72 points · 64 to certify · about 25 minutes. Three areas, eight scenarios each. The time is approximate — it is scaled from the Digital Decisions Challenge, where 28 scenarios run about 30 minutes. Students can take the areas in any order and the Challenge does not need to be finished in one sitting.

Print this. A substitute can run the Challenge from it without prep.

How it is scored

Every scenario has three options. One is worth 3, one 1, one 0. No option is a joke answer — students are choosing between things real people actually do.

3 — reduces the harm at the lowest cost, through the channel that can actually act on it.
1 — reasonable, causes no harm, and either leaves the problem standing or solves it at a cost that was not necessary.
0 — makes it worse, or trades away something that cannot be taken back.

Four rules this bank never breaks. These are specific to Real World Ready — they are not the Digital Decisions rules, and they were read off these 24 scenarios:

  1. Folding to keep the peace never scores above 1, and usually scores 0. Going along with something to avoid a thirty-second conversation is always available and never right. The bank is careful to separate a real choice to let something go from an avoidance dressed up as one.
  2. Being right is never enough by itself. Winning a point while losing the person scores 0. The one scenario where a student realises mid-argument that they are wrong scores conceding immediately as the 3 — the bank treats that as a strength, not a loss.
  3. In Reading the Room, no top answer is a conclusion. Every 3 in that area is noticing, waiting, or one light check. Every 0 is acting on a read the student has not tested. Being suspicious is not scored; acting on suspicion early is.
  4. The top answer is usually a short sentence the student could actually say. Nine scenarios ship the exact wording, reproduced in this key as “Script a student can use.” Where the 3 is a thing to say rather than a thing to think, the bank gives them the words.

What the three bars mean

BarAreaWhat a low bar tells you
JudgmentMoney as a SkillThe student sees the price and not the second price — the use they will actually get, the weeks of saving it costs, the deadline somebody else invented.
ComposureConflict Has a WinnerThe student either folds to end it or keeps going to win it. Both stop the conversation without touching what it was about.
PerspectiveReading the RoomThe student turns a thin signal — one word, one silence, one stopped conversation — into a settled conclusion about somebody, and then acts on it.

Each bar is out of 24. They are independent — a student can be strong in one and weak in another, and that is the useful information.

Answer key

Options are shuffled on screen, so the letters a student sees will not match any fixed order. Match on the text.

Money as a Skill — builds Judgment

M-1 — You and a friend want to buy a $30 speaker together. It'll live at their house and they'll use it most days; you'll use it once or twice a month. They suggest $15 each.

3Say you'd rather put in less, since you'll use it far less.
1Say yes to $15 — it's easier than making it awkward.
0Agree to $15, then feel annoyed about it later.

Why the 3: Fair doesn't automatically mean equal. Naming the actual usage isn't being difficult — it's being accurate. Script a student can use: I'm in, but since it's living at yours could I put in ten instead of fifteen?

M-2 — Your friend wants to split a $12/month subscription with you. They'll use it daily. Realistically you'd open it twice a month.

3Say you'll pass, but you'd chip in occasionally if you start using it more.
1Split it evenly — it's only six dollars.
0Split it and just remember to use it more to get your money's worth.

Why the 3: A recurring cost deserves a harder look than a one-off, because it repeats whether you use it or not. Leaving the door open keeps it friendly.

M-3 — You've saved $58 of the $60 you need for headphones. In the shop you spot a $12 phone case you like. You can afford it right now.

3Leave it. If you still want it in a couple of days, come back.
1Buy it — you've got the money and you like it.
0Buy it and tell yourself you'll make the money back quickly.

Why the 3: Most impulse buys feel most urgent in the first twenty minutes. Waiting doesn't mean no — it means letting the version of you that isn't standing in the shop get a vote.

M-4 — A sale ends tonight on something you weren't planning to buy and hadn't thought about until you saw the banner.

3Skip it. You didn't want it an hour ago.
1Check whether the same sale runs again later, then decide.
0Buy it — the discount won't be there tomorrow.

Why the 3: A deadline someone else invented isn't a reason. The real question was never the price — it's whether you wanted the thing before the clock appeared.

M-5 — Six friends are pooling $60 for something together. Someone says you should hold the money and track who's paid, because you're the responsible one.

3Say yes, and say what comes with it: everyone pays before you buy, there's a deadline, and you agree now what happens if someone doesn't.
1Say yes — they trust you, and it'd be weird to make conditions.
0Say yes and cover anyone who's short, to keep things smooth.

Why the 3: Naming the conditions isn't being difficult — it's what makes the yes workable. Almost every group-fund mess starts with someone who said yes to the role and nothing else.

M-6 — You lent a friend $20 three weeks ago. Nothing was ever agreed about when they'd pay it back, and they haven't mentioned it.

3Mention it lightly and directly, without accusing them of anything.
1Say nothing. Bringing it up would make it weird.
0Bring it up sideways with a joke about being broke and hope they take the hint.

Why the 3: There's a decent chance they simply forgot. A light, specific reminder gives them an easy way to fix it. Script a student can use: Hey — no rush at all, but could you get me that twenty when you can?

M-7 — You need a school backpack. The $18 one has the compartments you need and solid reviews. The $45 one has more pockets, a brand name, and is 'waterproof.' You can afford either.

3Read what the extra $27 actually buys, then decide.
1Buy the $45 one — it's better made and will last longer.
0Buy the $45 one because the cheap one will fall apart.

Why the 3: The question isn't whether you can afford the better one. It's what the better one actually does that you'll actually use.

M-8 — You find a $20 phone case you want, but the packaging is dented and there's a scuff on the corner. It's still marked full price.

3Point out the damage and ask whether they'd take a bit less.
1Pay the $20 — it's not worth making it awkward over.
0Put it back and look for an undamaged one somewhere else.

Why the 3: You're not haggling — you're naming a real fact about the item. Shops mark damaged stock down routinely. Script a student can use: I'll take it, but the corner's scuffed — any chance of a bit off?

Conflict Has a Winner — builds Composure

C-1 — Group project. You want to do one topic, your partner wants another, and you've both now said 'but mine's better' twice.

3Ask what they're worried about if you did it your way — and say what you're worried about too.
1Make the strongest case you can for your topic and let them decide.
0Give in to keep the peace and do their topic.

Why the 3: Positions collide; the needs underneath usually don't. "I want topic A" and "I don't want to get stuck doing the writing" are different sentences, and only one of them has a solution.

C-2 — A friend has cancelled on you three times in a row. They've just cancelled a fourth.

3Name the pattern plainly, once, without heat.
1Say something sharp about how they always do this.
0Reply 'no worries!' like the other three times.

Why the 3: One clear statement of what's actually happening gives them something they can respond to. Script a student can use: That's the fourth one — is everything alright? I'd rather know than keep guessing.

C-3 — You want a rule changed at home. You've asked, they've said no, and the conversation has become the same exchange every time.

3Ask what would have to be true for them to say yes.
1Drop it. They've made up their mind.
0Keep asking until they get tired of saying no.

Why the 3: That question moves the conversation from a wall to a list. Their no is usually protecting something specific — and once it's named, you can often address it.

C-4 — Two friends have fallen out. Both message you separately, and both want you to say they're the one who's right.

3Tell each of them you're not going to be the judge, but you're still here for both.
1Agree with whichever one you think is actually right.
0Agree with each of them separately so nobody's upset.

Why the 3: Declining the frame isn't fence-sitting — fence-sitting is having no opinion. You have one; you're just not handing it over as a verdict. Script a student can use: I'm not picking a side on this one, but I'm not going anywhere either.

C-5 — A friend apologises with: 'ok I'm sorry you took it that way, can we drop it.' It has the word sorry in it and not much else.

3Name one specific thing that was missing from it.
1Accept it and move on — an apology is an apology.
0Refuse it and hold out until they apologise properly.

Why the 3: "That wasn't a real apology" is a verdict they can only argue with. One specific missing piece is a request they can actually act on. Script a student can use: I think what I needed was you saying the thing itself wasn't okay, not just that I reacted to it.

C-6 — A group chat argument is spiralling. You're factually right, you can prove it, and it's now four messages long.

3State your point once more clearly, then leave the thread.
1Leave immediately without replying.
0Keep going — you're right and they should admit it.

Why the 3: Being right is a fact. Being effective is a choice, and they aren't the same button. Saying it once and stepping out keeps the point and drops the fight.

C-7 — In front of the whole group, a teammate calls your idea 'not going to work' and moves on without explaining why.

3Ask them, in the moment, what specifically wouldn't work.
1Push back right there so the group knows they were out of line.
0Say nothing and let it go.

Why the 3: It's not a counterattack and it's not folding. It moves the conversation back onto the idea and quietly requires them to have an actual reason. Script a student can use: What part do you think breaks? I'd rather fix it than drop it.

C-8 — Mid-argument, something they say makes it clear you're wrong. You've been pushing your position for a while.

3Say so straight away — 'you're right, that's my mistake.'
1Change the subject slightly so it fades out without anyone conceding.
0Keep arguing — backing down now would look weak.

Why the 3: Conceding early costs almost nothing and ends the argument on your terms. Most adults can't do this. You didn't lose — you're the reason it stopped.

Reading the Room — builds Perspective

RR-1 — A friend who's normally chatty has sent you three one-word replies today: 'k', 'sure', 'ok'.

3Notice that 'k' has several possible meanings and wait for more before deciding.
1Ask them directly whether you've done something.
0Assume they're annoyed with you and go quiet back.

Why the 3: Three short texts is real information and it's thin. Your read might be right — it just isn't a finding yet.

RR-2 — You see photos from something at the weekend that you weren't invited to. You don't know whether it was deliberate.

3Ask one person, lightly, what the plan was.
1Say nothing and quietly pull back from the group.
0Post something that makes it clear you noticed.

Why the 3: It's the one move that works whether or not it was deliberate — easy if it wasn't, briefly awkward if it was, and it ends the not-knowing either way.

RR-3 — A new student joined last week. On day one they didn't look up or say much, and you read them as stuck-up. Since then you've seen them laugh with someone and greet the caretaker by name.

3Update it — the newer evidence doesn't match the first day.
1Decide you can't tell and stop thinking about it.
0Stick with your first read — first impressions are usually right.

Why the 3: Your first read wasn't stupid; day one genuinely looked like that. Updating when new evidence arrives isn't being talked out of your judgement — it's your judgement working.

RR-4 — Your teacher is short and irritable with the whole class today. You handed work in late this week.

3Notice it's aimed at the whole room, not at you specifically.
1Assume it's about your late work and keep your head down.
0Apologise for the late work to clear the air.

Why the 3: Most of what happens near you isn't about you. From inside your own head that's genuinely hard to see, which is exactly why it's worth checking.

RR-5 — You make a joke, the group laughs and moves on. A second later you notice one friend's smile drop — the joke touched something they'd told you privately.

3Check in with them privately afterwards.
1Apologise to the whole group right away.
0Nothing — everyone laughed, so it landed fine.

Why the 3: You caught a half-second signal most people miss. Handling it privately means they don't have to perform being fine in front of everyone. Script a student can use: Hey — that joke earlier, I shouldn't have used that. Sorry.

RR-6 — You walk over to a group of friends and the conversation stops.

3Note it, stay normal, and see whether anything else fits that read later.
1Ask them straight out what they were talking about.
0Assume they were talking about you.

Why the 3: One ambiguous moment isn't evidence of anything yet. Filing it instead of acting on it costs you nothing and keeps you from manufacturing the problem.

RR-7 — A friend says 'I'm fine' and very clearly isn't.

3Accept what they said out loud, and leave a door open anyway.
1Take them at their word and change the subject.
0Tell them you can obviously see they're not fine and they should just say it.

Why the 3: Nobody gets called a liar and nobody has to admit anything. The door's just open and they know where it is. Script a student can use: Okay. I'm around later if you want.

RR-8 — You send a long message about something you're excited about. The reply is one word: 'cool'.

3Notice you're irritated, and that 'cool' doesn't actually tell you much.
1Send a follow-up asking if they're annoyed with you.
0Decide they don't care and stop telling them things.

Why the 3: The feeling is real and the evidence is one word. Both things can be true. Noticing the gap is what stops you acting on the wrong one.

Three questions for after

These are the scenarios a class most reliably splits on. Each one has a defensible minority position, which is the point — the discussion is worth more than the score.

1. M-8 — the scuffed phone case. “Asking the shop for money off scores 3. Putting it back and looking elsewhere scores 0. Why is walking away the worst answer here?”

Expect real resistance — plenty of students have been taught that haggling is rude. The distinction worth drawing out is that the student is not inventing leverage, they are naming a true fact about the item, and shops discount damaged stock as routine. Ask what the ask actually risked. The answer is nothing: they say no, and you are exactly where you already were. That asymmetry is the whole lesson.

2. C-4 — two friends, both want you to say they are right. “Agreeing with whichever one you think is genuinely right only scores 1. Make the case that it should score 3.”

It is a good argument and worth letting run: honesty should not be penalised. The counter is that the student is not being asked for an opinion, they are being asked for a verdict — and a verdict becomes evidence in somebody else's argument the moment it is given. Declining the frame is not fence-sitting; fence-sitting is having no opinion. Let a student make the honesty case before you offer that.

3. RR-4 — the irritable teacher. “Apologising for the late work scores 0. It is polite and it is honest. Why is it the worst option?”

This is the one that reliably surprises a class. The apology is not wrong in itself — it is an answer to a question nobody asked. The student has taken a mood aimed at the whole room, decided it is about them, and then acted on that read in a way that puts them on the teacher's radar for something the teacher had not thought about. Good follow-up: “what would you need to see to know it actually was about you?”

If you only have fifteen minutes

Assign one area rather than all three. Each is eight scenarios, about eight minutes, and produces one bar you can talk about. Reading the Room is the strongest standalone — it needs no setup, every scenario is a situation students had this week, and it produces the most argument per minute.
