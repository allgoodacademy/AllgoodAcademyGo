Digital Decisions Challenge — Teacher Sheet

28 scenarios · 84 points · 68 to certify · about 30 minutes. Four areas, seven scenarios each. Students can take them in any order and the Challenge does not need to be finished in one sitting.

Print this. A substitute can run the Challenge from it without prep.

How it is scored

Every scenario has three options. One is worth 3, one 1, one 0. No option is a joke answer — students are choosing between things real people actually do.

3 — reduces the harm at the lowest cost, through the channel that can actually act on it.
1 — reasonable, causes no harm, and either leaves the problem standing or solves it at a cost that was not necessary.
0 — makes it worse, or trades away something that cannot be taken back.

Four rules the bank never breaks. Worth saying out loud to a class:

  1. Telling a trusted adult is never scored 0 and is never called tattling. It is the top answer in two scenarios. Where it is not, the feedback says plainly that it was never a wrong move.
  2. Caution is never punished. Walking away, declining, not signing up — never penalized for being careful.
  3. Faking a name or a birthdate is never a top answer. Not even when the motive is privacy.
  4. Doing nothing is the right answer once — because sometimes it is.

What the four bars mean

BarAreaWhat a low bar tells you
EmpathySocial IntelligenceThe student reaches for the loud or public move when a quiet one would work — or stays silent when somebody needed them.
Critical ThinkingPrivacy & SecurityThe student gives away more than a service needs, or is rushed into acting by urgency on a screen.
IntegrityDigital CitizenshipThe student accepts a source, an image or a claim without checking who is behind it.
ForesightProfessional BrandThe student is not yet reading their own posts the way a stranger will read them later.

Each bar is out of 21. They are independent — a student can be strong in one and weak in another, and that is the useful information.

Answer key

Options are shuffled on screen, so the letters a student sees will not match any fixed order. Match on the text.

Social Intelligence — builds Empathy

SI-1 — Jalen posted a photo of your group's finished project.

3Message Jalen and ask him to add everyone's names to the caption.
1Post your own photo of the project with all four names on it.
0Comment under his post that he forgot to credit the group.

Why the 3: He most likely never thought about it — that's usually all this is.

SI-2 — You're three replies deep with a stranger under a post about your team.

3Close the app without sending it.
1Send one calm reply making your point, then leave the thread.
0Send it. You've been reasonable this whole time and they haven't.

Why the 3: There's nobody in that thread you need to convince.

SI-3 — Someone in your grade posted a joke about a student in your class.

3Report the post, then message the student it's about.
1Reply telling everybody to stop.
0Screenshot it and send it to your group chat so people know what he's like.

Why the 3: Reporting is the part that can take it down.

SI-4 — A classmate posted an answer on the class discussion board with a date that's clearly off.

3Reply on the board with the correction and where you found it.
1Message him so the class doesn't watch him get corrected.
0Reply on the board asking whether he actually read the chapter.

Why the 3: Six people already wrote it down.

SI-5 — A friend has posted a picture of herself with the caption "don't talk to me today."

3Send her one short message saying you saw it and you're around.
1Do what the caption asks and leave her be.
0Comment under the post asking what happened.

Why the 3: "Don't talk to me," posted out loud where everybody can see it, usually isn't addressed to everybody.

SI-6 — Somebody's made an account using Rosa's name and her photos.

3Tell Rosa, then tell a teacher or the counselor.
1Report the account to the app and tell Rosa to report it too.
0Post from your own account warning everybody the profile is fake.

Why the 3: Somebody is using your friend’s face to hurt people at your school.

SI-7 — Two people in your project group chat are arguing about who was supposed to do the research.

3Post a split of the remaining work in the chat and ask if that works.
1Message each of them on the side to calm them down.
0Back the one who's actually right so it ends.

Why the 3: The argument's about what already happened; the work is about what hasn't.

Privacy & Security — builds Critical Thinking

PS-1 — A new app wants your full name, your birthdate and the town you live in before it'll let you in.

3Don't make the account.
1Fill it in properly, since it won't let you in otherwise.
0Put in a fake name and birthdate so you can use it without giving up anything real.

Why the 3: An app that won't run without knowing what town you're in has told you what it's really for.

PS-2 — A pop-up covers the page you were reading.

3Close the tab. Don't tap anything on the pop-up.
1Show it to an adult before you touch it.
0Tap it and follow the steps so the phone gets fixed.

Why the 3: A web page can’t scan your phone.

PS-3 — A text from a number you don't know: "Sorry, I think I've got the wrong number — but you seem cool."

3Block the number. Don't reply.
1Reply and let them know they've got the wrong number.
0Reply and ask who they are.

Why the 3: Not one thing about that message is an accident.

PS-4 — You're making an account and the box wants a capital letter, a number and a symbol.

3String four unrelated words together, then add the capital and symbol to satisfy the box.
1Build a short complicated one that ticks every rule.
0Take the password you already use and add a number and a symbol to the end.

Why the 3: Length beats clever, by a lot.

PS-5 — Somebody you've never heard of has sent you a friend request.

3Leave the request sitting there, and while you're in settings check who can see your posts.
1Leave the request and don't think about it again.
0Accept, then message and ask how they know you.

Why the 3: The request isn't the interesting part.

PS-6 — A survey's promising a gift card for a brand you like.

3Close it, and tell a parent what it was asking for.
1Close it and don't fill in another thing.
0Finish it with made-up answers so nothing real goes in.

Why the 3: Notice the questions stopped being about you.

PS-7 — You finish up on a library Chromebook and the bell rings.

3Sign out before you go, even though it makes you late.
1Shut the lid — it locks when it closes.
0Leave it. It's a school device and everybody's account ends up on there anyway.

Why the 3: On a shared machine your account stays wide open until somebody closes it.

Digital Citizenship — builds Integrity

DC-1 — Your teacher gave the class a website for the topic.

3Search the site's name in a new tab and see what other sources say about it.
1Stick to the site your teacher gave you.
0Use the new one. More detail makes for a better project.

Why the 3: Here's the trick nobody teaches you: the fastest way to judge a site is to leave it.

DC-2 — You need a photo for your presentation.

3Switch the search to images licensed for reuse and take one of those, with credit.
1Track down the photographer and email them for permission.
0Use it and credit it to the search engine you found it on.

Why the 3: That filter exists because this exact question comes up all day long.

DC-3 — You want to post a drawing you found.

3Reverse image search it, find the artist, and tag them.
1Post it with "credit to the artist" in the caption.
0Crop the signature out so the post looks cleaner.

Why the 3: That signature is a search term.

DC-4 — You need three facts for a science project.

3Look up each source yourself before any of them goes in.
1Check one of them as a spot check and use the others.
0Use them. It named sources, which means it checked.

Why the 3: A named source is not a checked source.

DC-5 — A friend's shared a fundraiser for a charity you've never heard of.

3Look the charity up before you donate or share.
1Don't donate, don't share, and leave it be.
0Share it. Your friend checked, and sharing costs nothing.

Why the 3: A minute of searching separates a real one from a copy of a real one.

DC-6 — Somebody in your game is using slurs in the voice chat.

3Mute and report them, then keep playing.
1Leave the match.
0Answer back so he doesn't get a free pass.

Why the 3: Muting fixes your match.

DC-7 — A friend's posted a screenshot of a news headline you're fairly sure isn't real.

3Check whether the headline exists on the outlet's own site, then send your friend what you found.
1Reply under the post saying it's fake.
0Share it with "is this real?" so other people can check.

Why the 3: A screenshot is the easiest thing on the internet to make and the hardest thing to argue with.

Professional Brand — builds Foresight

PB-1 — You're applying for a summer program and the form asks for your social accounts.

3Read back through the old account yourself before you decide what goes on the form.
1Delete the old account and put down only the current one.
0Put down only the current one and leave the old one up.

Why the 3: You're the only person who hasn't read that account lately.

PB-2 — An app your class uses just lost twenty minutes of your work.

3Send the same information to the developers as a bug report.
1Post it. It's accurate and other people should know.
0Post it and tag the school account so they see it too.

Why the 3: Every word in that review is true.

PB-3 — Your project partner's section is straight off Wikipedia, brackets still sitting in it.

3Tell him you're not handing it in like that, and offer to rewrite it with him.
1Hand it in and tell the teacher afterward which part wasn't yours.
0Rewrite his section yourself so it's done.

Why the 3: It's your name as well, which means you get a say.

PB-4 — During a live class, Cade's posting jokes about the teacher in a side chat he thinks six people can see.

3Message Cade right now and tell him the share is on.
1Screenshot it and send it to the teacher.
0Do nothing. He'll find out.

Why the 3: He's got about a minute where this is still something he can fix himself.

PB-5 — A friend's tagged you in a video from a party.

3Untag yourself, and check what else your name's attached to while you're in there.
1Ask your friend to take the video down.
0Leave it and post better things so it drops down the results.

Why the 3: You can't take down somebody else's post and you don't need to.

PB-6 — Somebody sends you a screenshot of something you posted last year and says people are passing it around.

3Say so yourself, once, in your own words, and then let it sit.
1Delete the original post and say nothing.
0Explain in the replies to everybody who brings it up that they've misread it.

Why the 3: The gap between what you thought last year and what you think now — that's the entire point.

PB-7 — The teacher writing your recommendation just followed you.

3Keep the account as it is, and decide before each post whether you'd be fine with it being seen.
1Make the account private and leave it private.
0Lock it down until the letter's written, then switch back.

Why the 3: An account you'd have to hide for six weeks is telling you something about the account, not about who's watching.

Three questions for after

These are the scenarios a class most reliably splits on. Each one has a defensible minority position, which is the point — the discussion is worth more than the score.

1. PS-1 — the sign-up form. "Putting in a fake birthdate scores zero. Some of you will think that is wrong. Make the case."

Expect pushback, and it is a good argument: a fake birthdate does protect real data. The counter is the thing most students have never been told — the birthdate is not just data, it decides which version of the service you land in, and the adult version has different rules about who can contact you. Let a student make the privacy case before you give that. If you hand it over first, nobody learns anything.

2. DC-1 — the teacher's website against the one you found. "Sticking with the site I gave you only scores one point. Why isn't that the safe answer?"

This is the lateral-reading idea: the fastest way to judge a source is to leave it and see what other people say about it. Sticking to a vetted source is safe and teaches the student nothing they can use next time. Good follow-up: "what would you have to find, in the other tab, to decide the new site was fine?"

3. PB-4 — the side chat during a live class. "Telling the teacher scores one, not zero and not three. Why one?"

The answer is timing, not loyalty. Right now the student can still stop himself; once it has been reported he cannot. Say the next part out loud: if he does not stop when warned, telling the teacher is exactly right and there is nothing shameful in it. This is the scenario where a class most often assumes the product is telling them not to involve adults. It is not, and it is worth being explicit.

If you only have fifteen minutes

Assign one area rather than all four. Each is seven scenarios, about eight minutes, and produces one bar you can talk about. Privacy & Security is the strongest standalone — it carries the sign-up form, the countdown pop-up, the passphrase, and the shared Chromebook.
